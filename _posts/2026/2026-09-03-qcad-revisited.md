2 years ago, we [attempted](https://vtomole.com/blog/2024/02/13/qcad) to define a way for using quantum computers to help quantum computing's neighboring fields. We learned 2 important lessons since then. First we now know that classical computing is likely going to be enough enough for helping explore the [applications of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applications_of_quantum_mechanics) primarily due to [AI]() and [classical heuristics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11235). We have also learned that Another big change has occurred in 2 years; In APS 2026, Quantum information had the most presentations. 


Now that we have learned our lesson, what now? Well, let's say that quantum computing is best at studying the field of quantum computing. We are now in it for the pioneering spirit and as a result of gaining understanding, getting better and better at building the machines that will hopefully be useful in a few decades. Now, quantum information is not the small subfield of physics, but the dominant one, and self-sufficient one at that, With this new change, quantum information doesn't need to try to be of service to the once dominant condensed matter and particle physics subfields. 

Given these recent unprecedented changes of classical computer progress, and the field of physics being synonymous with quantum information, what is the purpose of industry in this new area? Why not leave the task of understanding quantum computing to academia and national labs?  Well, It’s normal for the free market to fund science, especially the sciences that we hope will pay off in decades. This is usually done in big industrial labs like IBM and Google, but there is a place for commercial quantum organizations that are not tied to big companies here. Quantum computing is the perfect mixture of the most prestigious scientific field, physics, and the wealthiest most influential scientific field, computer science. It seems safe to bet that the industry will remain funded for a long time without having commercial applications.

If quantum computing is likely to remain funded “no matter what”, the question becomes an ethical one: How do we choose to relate to ourselves in this quantum world? Do we choose to adopt the stance of vague corporate optimism, that is, hype, or do we adopt the stance of concrete optimism that great science will be had on the way, and work that was performed in a calm, steady, patient and sustainable way bear it's fruit in a few decades? This is a question for each to reflect on and decide.
